**Title:**
Reading, Numbers, and Noise: What I Learned from Cisneros, Children of the Sea, and AI

### Introduction: From “English Is Subjective” to Reading as Evidence

When I started this course, I thought of myself as a “math person” and English as a place where everything was subjective. My brain sees numbers, patterns, and averages when I look at information. I assumed that made me the wrong kind of student for a writing class. What I did not understand was that my analytical side is not a weakness in English. It is a tool, if I combine it with close reading and an honest look at my own beliefs.

Over the semester my instructor has repeated three connected ideas: read the text first, use what is actually on the page, and do not let AI or anyone else lie for you. Through Sandra Cisneros, Edwidge Danticat’s “Children of the Sea,” other readings like Louise Erdrich’s “American Horse” and Paula Gunn Allen’s “They Make Their Climb,” and through my own experiments with AI, I learned that reading carefully is a form of resistance. Stories can expose the same patterns of power, erasure, and propaganda that I see in statistics, genealogy records, and politics.

My claim is that this class helped me blend analytical thinking with subjective experience. By following a reading-first method, then using AI only to organize my thoughts, I learned to connect literature, family history, and current events into a more honest narrative about truth and lies in the world I live in.

### The Rhythm of the Syllabus: From Trayvon’s Hoodie to “American Horse”

At first the syllabus looked like nothing more than a calendar. As the weeks passed, I saw a pattern. We moved from *The Hate U Give* to Cisneros, then to writers like Danticat, Erdrich, Coates, and Gunn Allen. The sequence was not random. Again and again we faced texts about race, gender, colonialism, state violence, and people who are told their lives are worth less.

Even the syllabus cover mattered. My instructor chose a graffiti-style image of Trayvon Martin in a hoodie. That was not neutral decoration. It was a signal: this course would ask us to face uncomfortable truths about whose deaths are treated as tragedies and whose are treated as background noise. When we read Erdrich’s “American Horse,” I did not love every line, but I understood it as one more piece of a larger mural about stolen children and state power. When we read Gunn Allen’s “They Make Their Climb,” I could feel how Indigenous voices, like Black voices, have been pushed aside and are now speaking back.

At the same time, my instructor was strict about AI and plagiarism. She talked about students who clearly had not read the assigned chapters, who copied logs, or who let AI invent sources and scenes that did not exist. To her, that was not just “cheating.” It was a small version of the same habit that lets propaganda win: repeating what feels convenient instead of checking what is true.

Once I stepped back and looked at the whole rhythm, I realized the class is training us to be the kind of readers who do not just accept whatever story is easiest to swallow.

### Cisneros: Smoke, Names, and Who Gets Repackaged

Cisneros helped me understand how damage gets repackaged and sold back to the people who were harmed. In “Barbie-Q,” the girls buy smoky, half-melted Barbie dolls that survived a warehouse fire. They are so excited by the chance to own Barbie that they accept the warped legs and scorched clothes as part of the bargain. If a company finds a formula that makes people crave more sugar and carbs, and if there is more money in making people sick and then selling them medicine, we are right back in the smoke. Harm becomes a product. Suffering is baked into the business model.

Her struggle to rename herself echoes my own struggle with how others see me as a Black man. Reading closely, with page numbers and specific lines, forces me to slow down and see how her private discomfort connects to larger systems of race, language, and belonging.

### Children of the Sea: Ambiguous Death and the Cost of Not Knowing

Danticat’s “Children of the Sea” pushed me even harder. The story is told through letters between two young lovers separated by dictatorship. The young man writes from a crowded boat fleeing Haiti. The young woman writes from her village, surrounded by soldiers and violence. The government lies are always in the background: radio speeches, secret police, bodies that disappear in the night.

Danticat never gives these narrators simple names. That choice matters. They become stand-ins for all the people who are unnamed in history, and it forces me to pay attention to what they see and feel, not just what they are called. The ending is ambiguous. We do not get a clear answer about who survives. Instead we are left with images of the sea, the voices of the dead, and the young woman’s final words [insert short quote and page number from your edition of “Children of the Sea” here].

In class, my instructor walked us through specific sentences and asked whether the young man might still be alive, or whether he has already joined the “children of the sea.” I realized that without reading each line carefully, I would just be guessing. I would be treating life and death like a multiple-choice question.

This connects directly to my life. When a Black person dies at the hands of police, there are always two narratives: the official report, and the family’s memories and cellphone footage. If we accept the first version without reading the evidence, that person dies twice. Danticat trains me to pay attention not only to what is said, but also to what must remain unsaid because of fear. My instructor’s insistence on page numbers is practice for refusing lazy, one-sided stories in the real world.

### Genealogy and Statistics: Time Travel through Records

Outside class, I do genealogy. I study draft cards, enlistment records, census pages, adoption files, and DNA segment maps. My mind automatically starts clustering data: which relatives were drafted? Who disappeared from the records in the seventies? Which lines moved from Mississippi to Minnesota and then ran into new kinds of violence, like the destruction of Rondo in Saint Paul for the freeway?

Each record feels like a coordinate in time, like a start and end point on a DNA chromosome. Einstein wrote about time being relative, and Cisneros writes about talking to spirits. When I look at my great-grandfather’s draft card or my aunt’s death certificate, I feel both things at once. The past is gone, but it is still pressing against me.

Cisneros and Danticat do something similar with their stories. They give us fixed points in history: a burned-out warehouse full of dolls, a sinking boat full of refugees, a child taken by social services in “American Horse,” a climb up a mesa in Gunn Allen. My analytical side wants to chart these as data. The stories remind me that each point is a human life. The math and the narrative feed each other.

### English Teachers, Power, and My Own Bias

My relationship with English as a subject did not start in this class. My first English teachers in Minnesota were white, and those classrooms felt like power struggles. One memory that stays with me is reading *The Adventures of Huckleberry Finn* popcorn-style. The teacher had us go around the room, each student reading a paragraph. That meant we were going to hit the N-word over and over out loud.

I remember the tension when a white girl in my class reached that word. She hesitated, looked up at the teacher, and he nodded for her to go ahead. One by one, the white students said the word out loud, some of them stumbling, some of them almost enjoying the shock of it. The teacher stood at the front, conducting it like music, as if this “classic” American novel gave everyone permission.

Looking back, I see how that lesson was teaching more than literature. It was teaching my classmates that their voices and curiosity mattered more than my dignity. The curriculum became a shield. If a “naive” white boy named Huck said it, then repeating it was just “good reading,” and if I was hurt by hearing it, that meant I did not understand “great books.”

Because of experiences like that, there are times when I catch myself thinking or even saying “all white people” when I talk about racism and power. There is a part of me that feels my anger is justified because I am a victim of systems that were built against people like me. But this class has forced me to examine that reaction too. If I am going to demand that others see me as a full human being, I have to admit the ways I can slide into my own forms of prejudice and bigotry.

The point is not to pretend everything is equal or to “both-sides” oppression. The point is to be honest about how power works and how trauma can twist my thinking. It is true that white privilege is built into housing policy, school funding, policing, and the legal system. It is also true that if I let my mind flatten all white people into one category, I risk missing the complexity of who is actually my ally, who is my enemy, and who is trapped in the same machine but in a different position.

What I appreciate about Dr. D is that she does not ask me to respect whiteness. She asks me to respect truth. She flipped my idea of English on its head by treating the readings like evidence. In her class, English feels closer to math. A claim is either supported by the text or it is not. My “1” is true, my “0” is false. That kind of structure helps me sort through my own anger and decide where it belongs.

### Politics, Race, and the Privilege of Not Paying Attention

As I dug into the readings and my family history, I could not keep politics out. I think about relatives who left the South for supposedly safer places, only to have their new neighborhoods destroyed by freeway projects or “urban renewal.” I think about how my friend skipped voting and my nephew turned eighteen without ever casting a ballot. I had to tell my friend, “We are Black. We do not have the privilege of pretending politics is optional,” because the policies will reach us whether we touch the voting booth or not.

I also see how some white Americans can treat politics like a sports game. A white newscaster can say he skipped a historic civil rights event to go skiing and still benefit from the outcome of that struggle. That is part of what people mean when they say “white privilege.” It does not mean every white person is rich. It means the default settings of the system tilt in their favor, whether they notice or not.

At the same time, this class and my own reflections keep me from turning that into a simple “good group, bad group” formula. Power is real. Oppression is real. But so is my responsibility not to let bitterness become my only lens.

### AI, Deepfakes, and My Method

All of this affects how I think about AI. I can imagine a future where people use AI to create fake videos of political leaders or religious figures, fooling millions. If people already believe lies told in cheap commercials or sloppy news segments, how much easier will it be to trick them with convincing deepfakes?

That is why my method with AI is strict:

1. I read the assigned texts myself first.
2. I talk out my thoughts through voice-to-text, in my own messy language.
3. I give that raw narrative to AI and ask it to help me organize and clarify, not to invent new ideas or sources.
4. I check every reference against the actual books and add real page numbers.

In other words, I use AI like a file organizer, not a brain replacement. It helps me combine sentences, smooth transitions, and hold the structure, which fits my analytical style. But it does not decide what I believe about Cisneros, Danticat, or my own life. That part comes from reading, thinking, and being honest about my experiences.

If I stopped doing the reading and let AI “do English” for me, I would be just like the people who swallow whatever story the loudest voice is telling. Using AI within boundaries is my way of choosing not to be that person.

### Conclusion: Changing the Song

Earlier in the semester I asked myself if we are stuck in a bad song on repeat. Slavery, Jim Crow, mass incarceration, police violence, voter suppression, poisoned neighborhoods, deepfakes on the horizon: sometimes it feels like the same old melody with new lyrics.

This class has not magically fixed that, but it has given me tools. Cisneros teaches me to notice how harm gets repackaged as a bargain. Danticat teaches me to read the silences around official stories. Erdrich and Gunn Allen show me how colonial violence hits Indigenous families. Dr. D teaches me that English can be as precise as math when we use page numbers and evidence. My genealogy work and my political anger give me reasons to care, and my willingness to question even my own “all white” thoughts keeps me from becoming the thing I hate.

By combining my analytical mind, my lived experience, and a careful, honest use of AI, I am starting to write in a way that feels like me and still meets academic expectations. I still have work to do. But if I keep reading, checking, and telling the truth, maybe I can help change the song, even if only in my own small corner.
