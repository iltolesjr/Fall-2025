Essay 3 (Option A): Rhetorical Analysis
Title: What We Keep When Systems Take

Introduction
I grew up inside two kinds of rules. The first were family rules. Read every day. Speak up for yourself. Remember who raised you. The second were official rules. Papers. Forms. Case notes. A judge or a social worker who could change a house with one decision. When I read Louise Erdrich’s "American Horse," I felt the knot inside those two worlds. One set of rules says care for your child. Another says "remove" for their safety. The writing shows how those rules collide in real time. It hurts. It confuses. It leaves marks. This essay looks at how Erdrich stages state removal, how Paula Gunn Allen stages punishment for love, and how Maysan Haydar reframes the veil as choice. I argue that these authors use simple details, tight focal points, and steady voices to resist shame. They show how people keep bonds when systems push to break them. That is the key rhetorical move: make the human center feel close, make the policy sound far, and let readers see the cost of official actions.

Background and claim
The communities in these texts face stressors that repeat across America. Police removals. Poverty. Rules about bodies and love. Rules about dress. Rules about what mothers must be. "American Horse" shows a child taken from his mother under state procedures. Gunn Allen shows two girls whose friendship becomes love inside a culture with strict rules. Haydar explains how the veil can be a personal choice that resists public pressure. The claim here is simple. Each writer uses proximity who we stand next to whose voice we hear to undo the power of distant rules. Their craft pulls us close to a mother to two girls to a woman setting her own terms. That closeness is the tool. It makes readers weigh the policy against the person.

Erdrich removal salvage and focalization
Erdrich scene of removal feels cold because the language of salvage is cold. Officials say they salvage the child like a part from a wreck. That word hides pain. The mother Albertine hears the state plan as a fact yet the boy body and voice carry the truth. The craft choice is focalization through the child and mother not through the official. We see hands we see a door we feel a small grip on fabric. Big words are rare here. The page is built out of short actions. Open. Step. Lift. Cry. This is how Erdrich keeps the reader inside the room. The policy speaks in nouns. The family speaks in verbs. That contrast is rhetorical. It shows how procedure can erase care by changing language first.

Erdrich also uses pacing as a tactic. The scene slows at key moments. A pause before a signature. A pause before the car door closes. Small beats make each motion heavy. The writer understands that time inside a removal moves differently. It stretches. It snaps. That pacing invites readers to consider consent. Who is asked. Who is not. Who is carried. Who is silenced. In that way the page asks the same question the community asks is this help or is this harm. The answer is not neat. That is honest.

Gunn Allen rules about love and what punishment looks like
Paula Gunn Allen story of Elena and Ephanie puts two girls at the center and then holds a line about rules Elena was taught that same sex love is the devil. Ephanie was not. The text is a study in pressure versus grace. Gunn Allen craft uses parallel scenes of closeness eyes hands private words and then sets them against public words from parents and church. Here the rhetorical move is contrast how gentle love reads against loud doctrine. Gunn Allen does not scold the girls. The prose stays calm and plain. That tone is a form of care. It rejects shame while naming the rule. In analysis terms the writer lets the reader observe a culture discipline without adopting its voice. That choice matters. It lets readers see how rules can be carried in a person body as fear. It lets readers feel how a rule can split a friendship.

Gunn Allen also writes memory as a tool. Memory replays scenes but changes their meaning under stress. When Elena pulls away memory becomes a weight. When Ephanie remembers memory becomes proof. The text suggests that the punishment for violating a rule is not just public. It is internal. You punish yourself. You hide. You edit. By keeping the prose simple Gunn Allen leaves room for the reader to feel the cost without instructions. That is strong rhetoric. It creates space.

Haydar the veil as agency and audience correction
In Veiled Intentions Maysan Haydar chooses a direct voice. She speaks as a person not a theory. The veil is her decision. She explains how outsiders read the veil as submission while insiders can read it as self rule. The piece is built on clear statements and small scenes. The choice to veil shows modesty on her own terms. It shows a way to move in public that resists a certain gaze. The rhetoric works because it flips the frame. What looks like oppression from outside can be chosen resistance from within. Haydar invites readers to stop speaking for her and listen instead. That is audience correction. It points the argument at us gently.

Haydar also uses analogy to fix misunderstandings. She compares the veil to other dress codes and modesty norms. School uniforms. Office attire. Church clothes. This tactic reduces the distance between Muslim woman and the rest. It dissolves exoticism. It replaces foreign rule with familiar rule. In effect the text argues all communities have dress expectations this is mine I hold the key. This line of reasoning adds balance to the course theme on misogyny and islamophobia. It reminds readers that agency can live inside a practice that outsiders judge.

Bringing them together what the three texts teach
Across these works one thread repeats. Systems write rules. People live under them. The writers respond by keeping the person close. Erdrich stays in the room when removal happens. Gunn Allen stays with the girls when a rule about love falls like a wall. Haydar stays with herself when public opinion tries to speak for her. This is not a small choice. It is the core rhetorical method. Proximity is a counter power. It makes it harder for distant language policy doctrine stereotype to erase the human.

Each writer also chooses words that match the life being described. Erdrich avoids theory and holds simple actions. Gunn Allen avoids scolding and holds calm memory. Haydar avoids apology and holds a straight line about choice. Readers get a map of care. Care in crisis. Care in friendship. Care in dress. These maps invite one action replace shame with precision. Name what is happening. Ask what it costs. Choose language that does not hide pain.

Personal connection
My own life carries echoes of these pages. I grew up around case files and family warnings. I learned that some help can hurt. I learned that some rules protect and some rules shame. I have seen how a label on a form becomes a plan against a person. I have felt how easy it is to judge a practice from far away and how wrong that can be. Reading these texts pushed me to slow down. To ask more questions. To watch the hands not just the policy. To listen to the person who lives under the rule before I speak.

Current context and brief research tie in
Today child welfare agencies state removal policies aim at safety but also face bias and uneven impact on Native families. Public reports show higher removal rates for Indigenous communities compared to others. Civil rights groups still debate how to reform procedures to prevent harm while providing help. In LGBTQ contexts rules about bodies and love have changed in law but social practice can still punish quietly in families and schools. For Muslim women public debate about veiling rises and falls with news cycles yet personal agency remains the core claim inside communities. These realities match the course lens colonization policing misogyny and the work of repair.

Conclusion what we keep
The three writers make one strong promise. Our communities can rebuild if we keep the person at the center. The craft shows how. Use close focal points. Use simple words. Slow down the scene. Show the quiet cost. Then use that view to change policy practice and habit. Erdrich Gunn Allen and Haydar do not let official rules define the whole story. They hold a mother. They hold two girls. They hold a woman choice. This is the model for us. When systems take we keep what makes us whole. We keep names. We keep hands. We keep consent. We keep choice. That is how we undo shame and rebuild trust. That is how we move from violence to care.
