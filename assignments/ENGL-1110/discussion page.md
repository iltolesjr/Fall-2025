Week 1, Introductions and "The Hate U Give"
Introductions: For week 1 our main work is to introduce the course, our work, and ourselves, as we are becoming a community of learners this summer. The syllabus introduces the course, and our work will start with Angie Thomas' chapter from HUG (The Hate U Give), which I will address in my second paragraph here, but to introduce ourselves I'm asking folks to post a line or two about you - something you want classmates and me to know about yourself.  Usually, in a face-to-face class, I'd ask extroverts to start us off :) then I'd be last, and I'd still like outgoing folks to jump in, but since I'm posting this, I'll share my intro here:

I grew up in Boston and moved here in 1990 after my first teaching job in Lesotho, Southern Africa. Because my first teaching was under South African Apartheid (legalized white supremacy), because my parents grew up in public housing, and because I am a second-generation Irish immigrant, I have been trying for 30 years to build Decolonizing, anti-racist courses and curriculum. This work is taking so much longer than I ever imagined. 

* So please, post a 2-3 line intro yourself for your attendance points this week.


The Hate U Give, overview: So, Thomas begins with a girl returning home with her mother after fleeing "riots" and protests in her neighborhood. We learn later her name is Starr, nicknamed Munch, and her friend Kahlil had been killed by police at a traffic stop. But those plot details come out later, what Thomas does in this chapter is starts at a police roadblock showing the deep fear she and her mother have due to police brutality. The second page has 3 lines in italics: "Keep your hands visible, no sudden moves, only speak when spoken to"(165). Who speaks the lines? When did Starr first hear them," and why does Thomas share them - to say what to readers?

Then Starr and Lisa, her mom arrive home and Maverick, her dad, meets them in the driveway - he's worried about them and it shows. He the takes her with him to restock his store and shares lyrics from Tupac's "Keep ya Head up," information on the Black Panthers and the Nat Turner rebellion of 1831, and more of his own story being gang-affiliated and locked up. Why? What is Thomas doing by sharing this information in the middle of the chapter? Look especially at pages 170 and 171 where Maverick breaks down the history of social injustice crushing Black America, a historical oppression that continues today. Then, finally she ends chapter 10 with Starr listening to her parents argue about whether they should eave or stay in their neighborhood. If they split over this decision Starr says, "That's one more thing one fifteen takes from me"(181). Who is 115, and in this chapter that begins with a police roadblock and ends with 115 breaking her family, what is Thomas saying through Starr in this chapter?

Week 2: Edwidge Danticat's "Children of the Sea"
Our second reading, Edwidge Danticat's "Children of the Sea" - is a challenging piece that takes a few pages to connect with and then breaks our hearts. While Angie Thomas also breaks our hearts with the ongoing police brutality acted on Black and brown communities, asking us ultimately to speak up to end this violence, Danticat tells an even broader story. Set in Haiti during a political coup (she mentions the 'macoutes' which a google search will show are the Tonton Macoutes, secret police for "Papa Doc" Duvalier during his violent dictatorship), she tells of 2 youth fleeing the soldiers' unspeakable violence. Only Danticat does speak it - and so we must ask why? Why does Danticat tell of the sexual assaults and murders, seen in Celianne's story, the refugees fleeing in small leaky boats, the loss of lives on the boat and on land, the neighbor woman, Madan Roger, who loses her son to the soldier's violence, and is later beaten to death by them? Why does she tell of butterflies carrying news, of the girl fleeing with her parents to the countryside, and the boy - read the last 3 paragraphs of his section on page 111 - what happens to the boy? And finally, on page 112 the girl is speaking and she repeats almost exactly the very first line of the story: "behind these mountains are more mountains and more black butterflies still and a sea that is endless like my love for you" (112). What does Danticat say through this repetition; what are the mountains and what is the sea? Ultimately what is Danticat's purpose with this story?  At first we are lost as readers with no names, no dates, no history, as if Danticat puts us in the tiny boat, and in the girl's home, even right in their heads - why?

As always, no need to answer all these questions - answer what you can, ask what you like, comment on a peer's post, and email if you need to. Thanks, Dr. D.

Week 3, Gish Jens' "In the American Society"
Just as Danticat managed her large story about Haitian refugees and people displaced by military violence, with two narrative voices, the boy and the girl, Jen uses two sections to manage her story of two Americas, part 1 and part 2.


Danticat connected Haitians lost as refugees at sea to African people who were earlier lost to the sea of the Middle Passage: “I go to them now, the children of the deep blue sea, those who have escaped the chains of slavery to form a world beneath the heavens and the blood-drenched earth where you live”(111), and she give us hope despite extreme suffering in the “seas of endless love” with which the girl holds the boy’s memory, and with which we readers can hold the memory of all lost to colonizing violence.


And Jen too gives us hope in the family leaving the country club party, the party of white wealth, and following their own light in the darkness of America. Before that hopeful ending, what is Jen’s focus of part one, “In His Own Society,” and what the focus of part two “In the American Society?” What happens to Ralph Chang, the father, business owner, descendant of village rulers in China, Chinese immigrant when he can’t bribe the court to help his undocumented workers? And through the character of Jeremy – a man so wealthy he takes a 6 month vacation EVERY YEAR, what does Jen say about white privilege? Week 4, Oscar Hijuelos' "Visitors, 1965"
Our 4th reading this week, Hijuelos' "Visitors, 1965" adds another perspective to the immigration stories and American identity stories we have read so far. One thing you may begin noticing is how writers manage larger chunks of information; where Thomas had Maverick deliver Black History on the Panthers and Nat Turner's slave rebellion, Danticat explained both refugee experience and those left behind with 2 narrators, and Jen told of dominance within Chinese families, but also the white dominance they have to manage in America by using 2 parts to her story.

Now Hijuelos gives us 4 sections - whew!  So he tells Alejo's individual experience immigrating 20 years earlier, then the backstory of Fidel Castro's Cuban revolution, then the son, Hector's story of not fitting in either with Cuban relatives or with American neighbors, and then the recent immigration of Pedro and his family, who come with connections, and who came from wealth, and so have a very different experience. So, a big, multi-generational immigrant story. Some questions to consider for your posts this week: Why is the clipping about Alejo serving dessert to the Russian leader Krushchev a "yellowed newspaper clipping?" (310). What does that say about his early dreams as new immigrant? Why does Hector describe his memories of his one visit to Cuba as a small child as, "the dream house of Cuba?"(317). And why does the piece end with Mercedes literally screaming: "what about the pennies I saved? What about us?"(325). And who is the "us" Hijuelos is screaming for through Mercedes?  As always, answer some of these or pose your own questions on Reading #4, "Visitors, 1965."

Week 5: Essay 1 Workshop
So here we are in the midst of writing our first Essay for the course. As you may have learned in high school and other English courses, there are many ways to start a paper, all of which depend on the context or field you are writing in, and the goals you have for that writing. In Biology and other Science writing, clearly laying out a problem you will explore or an approach you will test out is most common. While in the Humanities we value human stories and use them to engage, or draw-in readers. This is why I use a "narrative" introduction - a story from your life or one you've seen in the media through which you can invite people in, to your paper. Sometimes people begin by defining terms or pulling quotes from other sources, but these are outside the writer's experience, so starting with a narrative often gives your paper a voice of authority as you know what you have lived. This approach also begins with confidence, as that and a sense of authority are often valued in academic writing.

Once you have your intro, you can then use your reading log and explain and analyze what one of our readings was trying to convey, using quotes from the reading as supporting evidence. Strong drafts on Thomas's writing point out that Starr tells Maverick "I can't be silent," which shows that one central message in Thomas' work is we can not end police brutality and white supremacist culture without speaking it out - all of us. And powerful Danticat drafts look at her repeated line from the very beginning and the very end: "behind these mountains are more mountains and black butterflies still and a sea that is endless like my love for you" (112). Danticat also gives us a way out of the deep sea of human misery in Haiti, the US, and worldwide following settler colonization: how do we help refugees and descendants of those lost to slavery's Middle Passage violence, the "children of the sea?" We have oceans full of human love to show each other if only we access it; we tell the stories of those lost, mourn their memories, show compassion and support to their descendants - those protesting for Black lives and ending the separation of families along our southern border and with excessive ICE deportations in many cities right now. 

And for Gish Jen, where Ralph Chang's male dominance causes him to lose employees, but that pales in comparison to Jeremy's white privileged dominance at the party. And Jen has the family leave the party - leave white dominance and follow their own light, the "bright white" of the father's shirt out of the darkness in America for immigrant families. We all gotta leave the white supremacist party as it is killing the country. So each of our stories tell of very contemporary issues with which most of us have had some experience, and each writer tells us how to heal, and get past these killing issues. 

So in Essay 1, tell a piece of your story, then connect it to one of our first four, pull quotes and give explanations from the writer's work, and end highlighting how they feel we can move past the violent history we have inherited. And for our discussion in week 5, tell us what has been the hardest part of getting the essay done, ask a question, or respond in some other way to my notes or your classmates' responses.

And as always stay healthy and well,

Best, Dr. D.

Week6: Alfred Lubrano's "Bricklayer's Son"
Just as Oscar Hijuelos had a "class critique," an analysis of how all immigrants don't migrate with equal resources, where some come from, and stay in poverty, while others have connections and mobility that make their lives easier. Lubrano also looks at class mobility between a son and his parents, where the father has "built a lot of places in New York City he can't get into" (1) once the walls go up. In this piece he looks at the folks he meets as he's a student at Columbia - middle class people who live lives of "cultural capital" - exposure to art, music, foods, practices, that working class people never learn about. Beyond that he writes of a belonging, a sense of "entitlement" that middle class, or "white collar" people carry, where they feel welcomed wherever they go. Working class or "blue collar" people do not know this always-welcome, and instead often feel excluded and undeserving.

He goes into his own working-class childhood in Brooklyn, describing how he never really fit in there either - the sexism, racism, roughness that he didn't fit easily. So, he really fits neither place - calls himself and folks like him a "straddler" set somewhere between blue collar and white-collar worlds. While he shows blue collar, working class communities are not perfect, he does begin with his father - showing respect for manual laborers, and then ends with his mother, of whom he says about her dreaming at night of back when she was a child and the double-dutch champion of her neighborhood: "reminding her she once had promise and dreams of her own" (14). So, both at the start and the end he writes lovingly of his parents, so what is he saying about working-class communities? What does he say about white collar workers? And what else stands out to you from Lubrano's "Bricklayer's Son?"

Week 9, Sandra Cisneros, "Barbie-Que"
Cisneros' story is a lovely 2-page gift after so many longer readings, but as I said before, don't miss its depth in the piece's brevity. Some things to note early on is her use of listing many accessories and items - what is her comment on American materialism - our drive for more things in this listing, and how we teach it to our children through toys? Do note these are two little girls, as in their repeated story of "steal the boyfriend" one says, "oh no, you stinky" (252), so they are maybe 5 or 6, and already they see adult women fighting over men - drawing value more from having a man than seeing value in themselves. And what class critiques does she offer as the girls get many of their Barbies from a fire sale, where they "wash and wash and wash" them? 

The final paragraph in this brief piece is especially full as the speaker says, "so what?" three times in one paragraph, which is a rhetorical device used often in argument; what is she defending herself against? And the final line describes a doll with a melted foot who she dresses in a "Prom Pinks" outfit and about who she asks, "so long as you don't lift her dress, right? -who's to know"(253). So, what does lifting a prom dress" suggest? And what do little girls and women learn we must hide to be acceptable in American society?

Week 11: Reaing #9, Louise Erdrich "American Horse"
"Buddy had been knocked awake out of hiding in a washing machine while herds of policemen with dogs searched through a large building with many tiny rooms. When the arm came down Buddy screamed because it had a blue cuff and sharp silver buttons.  ... There was something coming and he knew it" (210). This is how Louise Erdrich starts her story of a Native mother and son hiding from police, and yet they are in fact in a small shed with only 2 police and 1 social worker hunting them. So Erdrich starts with what some call, cultural memory, which is the idea that we each carry in our genetic material memories from our ancestral line. For Native American people part of that memory is 100 years of child-removal into violent, often deadly, boarding schools which later became removal into foster and adoptive homes - often white homes. She shows that removing one child now, is connected to those hundreds of removals during colonization.  Why does she tell the story from Buddy's perspective? What does she say through what he sees, experiences, suffers?

Then in the final pages Erdrich, though telling a violent story of the state breaking a family, a people,  she shares poetic, beautiful imagery giving a perspective that seems impossible given such violence. As the tribal officer, Harmony, raises his gun toward her, Erdrich writes, "She and Harmony stood within feet of each other, breathing. Each heard the human sound of air going in and out of the other person's lungs. Each read the face of the other as if deciphering letters carved into softly eroding veins of stone. Albertine saw the patterns of tiny arteries that age, drink, and hard living had blown to the surface of the man's face. ... She saw the black veins in the wings of the butterfly, roads burnt into a map, and then she was located somewhere in the net of veins and sinew that was the tragic complexity of the word so she did not see Officer Brackett and Vickie Koob rushing toward her, but felt them instead like flies caught in the sme web, rocking it"(219).- Whew, so what is Erdrich saying here, and just below this quote on page 219 when she says, "her father's hand was on her chest and shoulders lightening her," what does that mean as well?

Then finally, in this heartbreaking and heart-opening story, that ending where Buddy tries to thank the social worker for the candy, but instead a "great rattling scream and then another, rip out of him like pieces of his own body" (220) - what does Erdrich say about child removal, especially for Native children with this final line?

So yes, another truly heart-rending story, but like with Danticat and so many others - there is hope here if we can listen to it, feed and grow it; just as Albertine feels the hand of her father (who has passed) on her chest, Buddy too will have the spirit of his mother, as all Native children and in fact all children, have the ancestral spirits to draw from to make change, right past wrongs. European-descended people, descendants of colonizers, must end the colonizing systems still oppressing people of color in the US, while those whose people suffered colonization must tap into the strength of ancestors who survived that trauma. So there is hope here, but only if CHANGE comes. BTW - VOTE!!!!

Week 12: Paula Gunn Allens "They Make Their Climb"
Starts Nov 11
So our "penultimate" (second to last) reading this term is by Puebla\Guadalupe writer Paula Gunn Allen who not only shares stories from her marginalized Native culture, but what some would call a "Two-Spirit" identity, a term of gender variation, which Cameron defines as, "an Aboriginal-specific term of resistance to colonization and non-transferable to other cultures" and Lang explains that "for Aboriginal people, their sexual orientation or gender identity is secondary to ethnic identity. ... at the core of contemporary two-spirit identities is ethnicity, an awareness of being Native American as opposed to being white or being a member of any other ethnic group."(Wikipedia)

You will notice this reading has 2 pages of biographical/cultural info before the actual story. As Gunn-Allen is from an underrepresented group, this added info can be very helpful - especially the notes on Keresan spiritual belief and origin stories - stories that center women: twin sister spirits, Yellow Woman and The Woman Who Fell From the Sky. This centering of women in origin stories and community leadership is still almost shocking in our colonial settler nation where male-dominance is still so normative.

To understand especially the ending of Gunn- Allen's story do remember: "Yellow woman ... strays beyond the safe spiritual and physical boundaries of the pueblo, usually to the ultimate enrichment of the people." And "The Woman who fell from the sky - fell and survived."

So what does Gunn-Allen say through the ending? And she shares 2 tales - one from Elena's Chicano people, then one from Ephanie's Guadalupe people, both of young women who jumped to their deaths from the mountain for violating the tribe's beliefs. Why does she share these tales and how do they connect to Elena and Ephanie's climb? Ultimately what is Gunn Allen saying through the story? There's a detailed description of eating an orange - what's that about? And yes - it is sexual, so notice your own hesitation, embarrassment, for some of us even shame with this image - what does Gunn Allen say here about internalized stereotypes around sexuality in readers, and about the "danger of falling" in homophobic societies?

As always, email any questions and take good care,

Week 14: Essay 3 Writing Workshop
Starts Nov 25
Hello all, so as with Essays 1 and 2, our DB post this week is a chance to discuss any challenges or improvements coming up as we write the 3rd and final new essay for the course.  There were some great posts about how your reading and use of reading logs has changed after writing Essay 2 - great insights! With Essay 1, I focused my attention on introductions - how you begin a paper. Then in Essay 2 I gave a lot of feedback on transition and use of quotes, the evidence section of an academic essay. Here with our last new essay, I want to focus on some options in your conclusion - the final section of your work. 

So often writers are exhausted by the end of the paper, and the closing can read that way, but we need to add some energy and strength to that final section so that readers - especially grading readers while you are in school - feel the paper finishes with as much strength as it started. One good way to do that is to add some brief, recent research. I know - in high school we are told to add no new information in your conclusion, but instead wrap up and restate your main points. But in fact the best writing would never only repeat, but always builds upon and adds to what has already been shared. So, to that end my guidelines for stronger conclusions include the following:

1. Refer back to your intro as a way to cue the reader you are closing. So if you began a paper on Sandra Cisneros' "Barbie-Q" describing the pressures you felt as a child to conform to specific gender roles, maybe the "boys don't cry rule, or girls forced to wear pink, you can restate that in the start of your conclusion as a cue you are ending: Just as my uncles teased me for being too sensitive, boys are still told they must "man up" and never show emotion. Cisneros looks at the damage to girls' self-esteem such gendering of girls through Barbie dolls can cause ...

2. Restate the author's main purpose; be sure you do not end only on your own experience, but as an academic essay you are analyzing - making sense and meaning of a story or an issue. (see my second sentence in point 1 above)

3. Add some brief, recent research on the topic to make the issue timely. You can do this with brief google searches of any of the topics our readings discussed: police brutality, support for refugees, immigrant exclusion, violence surrounding Black communities, accepting Native identities, ending-child removal of Native children,rejecting homophobia, ending stereotypes about veiled Muslim women, stopping the patriarchal gender oppression of girls and women, as searching the topic with the recent year attached allows you to find recent statistics or examples: "toys and gender issues, 2025; girls, social messages about femaleness and self-esteem, 2025," "Veiling and anti-Muslim bias, 2025," "police brutality, 2025"etc. 

And a final note on using "In conclusion," - it has been so overused over the years it is really just better writing to avoid it and instead use that reference to your own intro as a cue the paper is ending.

So as always raise any questions or share comments on these notes or add a response to a post of a classmate, and be well,

Dr. D.
